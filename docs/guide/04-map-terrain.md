# The Map & Terrain

The New World is your board, your larder and your battlefield all at once. Every colony you found, every road you cut and every war you fight happens on this map, so a governor who reads the land well has an edge before a single musket is fired. This chapter explains how the map is laid out, what the different kinds of ground are worth, how terrain speeds or slows your units, how the map is revealed as you explore, and — most usefully — how to read a stretch of coast and pick a good place to settle.

## How the map is laid out

The world is a grid of tiles. Most of it is one of two things: **land**, where your colonists live and work, and **ocean**, which your ships cross and your fishermen work. A rim of water wraps the landmass, so every continent has a coast.

Along the far **east and west edges** lies the **high seas** — the deep open ocean a ship sails into to reach Europe and to come home again. The high-seas line isn't a fixed row of columns; it hugs the shape of the coast, staying a few tiles clear of any land, and there is always a way out on each side. A voyage to Europe or back takes a few turns once your ship reaches this water (see [Trade & the Europe Screen](12-trade-europe.md)).

Depending on the setup you chose in [Starting a New Game](02-new-game.md), the land may be a single great **continent**, a handful of large **islands**, or many small ones — and you may be playing a freshly generated world or the fixed map of the Americas. Either way, the land is carved into named **regions** — polar caps, the Pacific and Atlantic seas, mountain ranges, rivers, and stretches of open country. The first power to explore a region "discovers" it and earns score for it, so exploration pays (see [Exploration & Discovery](06-exploration.md)).

## Terrain types and what they are worth

Terrain follows climate. The hot, wet equator grows lush **savannah and tropical forest**; dry bands become **desert and scrub**; the frozen poles are **arctic ice, tundra and boreal forest**; and the temperate middle holds the classic mix of **plains, grassland, prairie, marsh and forests**, with **hills and mountains** rising in ranges across it.

Each terrain has its own character:

- **Open country** (plains, grassland, prairie, savannah) is easy to cross and good for farming — grain, and cash crops such as tobacco, cotton and sugar depending on the climate.
- **Forests** yield furs and lumber but produce less food. A pioneer can clear one to turn it into the farmland beneath.
- **Hills and mountains** are poor for food but rich in **ore** and, in the mountains, precious **silver** — the makings of tools, muskets and a full treasury.
- **Marsh, desert, scrub and the polar wastes** are the harshest ground: low yields all round. You can settle near them, but you would rather not depend on them.

!!! tip
    Every terrain is good at *something* and weak at something else. A strong colony sits where several **different** terrains meet, so one town can farm, log, mine and fish at once.

Exact yield figures for every terrain and good live in the in-game **Colopedia** — press its key at any time, or see [Reference & Colopedia](22-reference.md). This chapter keeps to the character of each terrain; the Colopedia has the numbers.

### Bonus and special resources

Scattered across the map are **special resources** — a fur-rich forest, a prime tobacco field, a rich mineral lode. A tile carrying one produces noticeably more of its signature good, which makes such tiles prizes worth building a colony around.

Some resources are **finite**: mineral deposits, ore and silver hold a set amount that **runs down as you mine it**. When the lode is exhausted the bonus vanishes and the tile falls back to its ordinary yield — so a silver rush is a windfall, not a pension. Most other resources (prime grain, furs, sugar and the like) are limitless and never run out. Clearing a forest can even **uncover** a hidden resource on the fresh ground beneath.

## Getting around: movement, roads and rivers

Every unit has a number of **movement points** it spends each turn (see [Units & Movement](05-units-movement.md)). Terrain sets the price of each step:

- **Open ground** is cheap — a unit crosses plains and grassland quickly.
- **Rough ground** — forests, hills and mountains — costs more, so a march through the mountains is slow.
- A unit with any movement left may always make **one** step, even onto costly ground, but it uses up its whole turn doing so.

Two things turn the map into fast lanes:

- **Rivers.** Moving *along* a river — from one river tile to the next — costs only a fraction of a normal move, so a unit can travel far by following a waterway. Stepping off the river, or onto it from dry land, costs the usual amount. Rivers also make the land beside them richer, which is why colonies cluster along them.
- **Roads.** Your pioneers build roads on any land ([Founding a Colony](07-founding-colony.md) and the colony chapter cover pioneering). A road tile connects to the next just like a river — cheap to travel — and roads and rivers link into one another, so a road bridging two rivers makes a highway across your territory.

!!! warning
    Stepping a **land** unit onto a tile it has never seen before ends its turn on the spot, however much movement it had left — the wilderness is slow to explore. Moving across ground you already know costs only the normal amount, so scouting the unknown is always the expensive part of a journey. Ships are exempt.

## Fog of war: revealing the map

The world starts **black**. Your units and colonies light it up: each reveals the tiles around it — a lone colonist sees the ring immediately around it, a **colony** sees further, and a **scout** (a colonist on a horse) sees further still. Once you have seen a tile it stays on your map forever.

With **fog of war** on — the classic default — a tile you have explored but can no longer see is shown **dimmed and "remembered."** You see the land as you last left it, but anything moving there now (a rival's ship, a native war party) is hidden until you look again. Turn fog off at setup and every explored tile stays fully visible for the rest of the game, so you keep a live window on all the ground you have ever walked. Either way, land you have never reached stays black until you go there.

## Reading terrain to pick a settle site

Choosing where to plant your first town is the most important map decision you make. Study the tiles around a candidate spot before you build:

- **Sit on the coast.** A coastal colony can build ships, ships can reach it, and the sea feeds it — a water tile hugging the shore yields more **fish** than open ocean, and the water beside a **river mouth** is richer still. A coastal town is also your link to Europe.
- **Prefer a river tile.** A river enriches several goods and gives your units a fast lane in and out.
- **Look for variety.** The best sites have several terrains within reach — farmland for food, forest for furs and lumber, and hills nearby for ore. One town that can do everything beats one that can only farm.
- **Note the special resources.** A prime field or a rich lode in the surrounding tiles is a strong reason to settle there — just remember the mineral ones will one day run dry.
- **Mind the rough ground.** Mountains and marsh feed no one, but a mountain range at your back is also a wall an enemy must climb.

!!! tip
    You do not have to get the perfect spot on turn one. Found a solid coastal town first, learn the surrounding country as the fog lifts, and place your later colonies to fill the gaps — the mine near the ore, the logging town in the deep forest.

With the land read and a site chosen, you are ready to plant your first colony — read on in [Founding a Colony](07-founding-colony.md).

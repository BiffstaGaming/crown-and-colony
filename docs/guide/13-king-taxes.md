# The King, Taxes & Your Monarch

You did not sail to the New World alone. Back in the mother country your **King** watches over your colonies, and he is anything but a silent partner. He taxes your trade, drags you into wars, occasionally does you a favour — and, all the while, quietly builds the army he will one day send to crush you if you ever reach for independence. This chapter explains what the Crown can do to you, what you can do about it, and how his growing demands push your own colonists toward rebellion.

## Your relationship with the Crown

Once your colonies are a few decades old, the King begins to take an interest. Each turn he weighs his options, and most turns he simply does **nothing**. But as the years pass he grows bolder and his interventions come more often. Early in the game — and any time you hold no colonies at all — he leaves you entirely alone. He also stops meddling once you have **declared independence**; from that point you answer to no crown but your own.

The King acts at most **once per turn**. When he does act, one of a handful of things happens: a tax demand, a royal offer, a decree of war or peace, a gift of troops or gold, or a quiet reinforcement of his home army. Two of these — a tax rise and a mercenary offer — are **decisions you must answer** in a dialog. The rest simply happen, and you are told about them in your end-of-turn messages, because in the classic game these are the Crown's prerogative and you get no say.

## Tax hikes: pay or throw a tea party

The most familiar royal intrusion is the **tax demand**. The King announces a proposed new sales-tax rate and names the goods he has his eye on — usually your most valuable stockpile. You then choose:

- **Pay the tax.** Your sales-tax rate rises to the new figure. It stays there, eating a share of every future sale in Europe, for the rest of the game (unless the King later softens — see below).
- **Refuse, and hold a tea party.** Your colony dumps the offending goods into the sea in defiance. The tax is **not** raised — but that good is now **boycotted**, and you cannot sell it in Europe until you settle the arrears. In return, your colonists' rebel sentiment **surges**: liberty-bell output jumps for a good while before slowly fading back down.

This is the central trade-off. A tea party keeps your taxes down and fires up the revolution, but it costs you the ability to trade a whole class of goods. Refuse to boycott your bread-and-butter export and you may cripple your own economy.

!!! warning
    If the demanded goods have already been sold or moved out of the colony by the time you answer, refusing does **not** trigger a tea party — the King simply forces the rise through, and adds a small penalty on top. To refuse meaningfully, the goods he named must still be sitting in the colony.

### Lifting a boycott

A boycott is not forever. Open the colony screen and you will find a **Boycotts** section listing each blockaded good, the back-tax owed, and a **Pay** button. Pay the arrears and that good is free to trade once more. The button is greyed out (with the price shown) when you cannot yet afford it. Later, electing the Founding Father **Jacob Fugger** lifts *every* boycott at once — see [Founding Fathers & the Continental Congress](14-founding-fathers.md).

### Taxes climb gradually

Here is a piece of good news. Once the King wins a tax rise, he **leaves your taxes alone for a while** before he can demand another. So your sales tax creeps upward over the course of a long game rather than being hammered to its ceiling in a few decades — expect it to sit somewhere in the middle range by the late game, not pinned at the maximum. You cannot dodge the wait by saving and reloading; the Crown remembers exactly when it last taxed you. (This is a deliberate, faithful-to-the-original tempering of the pace.)

!!! tip
    Your tax rate does double duty: it is also the **cut the King takes** to ship a treasure train home for you from a colony. The higher your taxes, the dearer that service — one more reason to keep the rate in check, or to sail your treasure home yourself. See [Natives & Diplomacy](15-natives-diplomacy.md) for treasure trains.

## The King's goodwill

The Crown is not always grasping. When the King is in a generous mood he may **lower your tax rate** by a few points — no decision for you, just a happier purse. He may also simply announce that he has **graciously waived** a tax rise this turn: a message only, with nothing else changed. Both arrive quietly and are noted in your turn events.

## Royal offers and free support

Beyond taxes, the King reaches into your wars:

- **Mercenary offers.** In wartime the King offers you a band of **veteran soldiers** for hire at a discount off the usual European price, trimmed to what your treasury can afford. Accept and they appear on your Europe dock; decline an offer you could clearly afford and the King turns cold — he will make **no further offers** for the rest of the game. He may also make a larger offer if you are sitting on a great pile of gold.
- **Free troops and gold.** Sometimes the Crown sends free soldiers or a **free warship** (after you have been raided by privateers) at no cost at all. When he declares a war for you, he may also fund it with a one-off force of veterans and a lump of gold — though a cautious King only helps when the enemy is at least as strong as you, and gives nothing when you clearly have the upper hand.
- **War and peace made for you.** The King can **declare war** on a rival European power on your behalf, or **make peace** for you — dragging you into or out of a conflict by royal decree. Electing **Benjamin Franklin** to your Congress stops the King declaring war for you (he can still make peace); see [Rival European Powers](16-rival-powers.md) and [Founding Fathers & the Continental Congress](14-founding-fathers.md).

## The gathering storm: the Royal Expeditionary Force

Every so often, with no announcement you can refuse, the King adds ships and soldiers to a great army back home. This is the **Royal Expeditionary Force (REF)** — the army that will cross the ocean to crush your rebellion the instant you declare independence.

The REF grows steadily throughout the game, and it grows **faster the more you provoke the Crown**. The longer you wait and the larger your empire, the mightier the force you will eventually face. You are warned each time it is reinforced. Balancing "one more turn of preparation" against "a larger enemy to defeat" is the central tension of the endgame — see [The Road to Independence](18-road-to-independence.md) and [The War of Independence](19-war-of-independence.md).

## How the Crown breeds rebels

There is a poetic justice to all this. Every royal imposition — every tax hike, every boycotted cargo, every insulting demand — hardens your colonists against the Crown. A tea party in particular sends **liberty-bell production surging**, and liberty bells are the very fuel of independence: they raise your colonies' **Sons of Liberty** membership toward the 50% you need to declare. The King's greed, in the end, is what turns his loyal subjects into revolutionaries. Manage him well, refuse him at the right moments, and you turn his own overreach into the spark of your freedom.

For how liberty bells and Sons of Liberty work, see [Founding Fathers & the Continental Congress](14-founding-fathers.md); for the exhaustive numbers on any good's price or arrears, consult the in-game **Colopedia** (`C`).

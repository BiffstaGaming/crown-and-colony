# Rival European Powers

You are not the only crown planting flags in the New World. Other European powers land their own ships and colonists, spread out along the coast, and set about building empires of their own. They can be trading partners, uneasy neighbours, or open enemies — and how you handle them shapes your independence race and your final score.

This chapter covers how you meet the rivals, what you can do at the negotiation table, and how their presence weighs on the rest of your game. For the native nations, see [Natives & Diplomacy](15-natives-diplomacy.md); for the mechanics of actual fighting, see [Combat: Land & Naval](17-combat.md).

## Meeting the other powers

You begin the game **uncontacted** with every rival — you simply haven't met them yet. Each power lands far away, on its own stretch of coast, and plays under its own fog of war. You won't see a rival's units or colonies until one of *your* units, scouts, or colonies brings their tile into view.

The moment either side first spots the other, you become **at peace** automatically. Meeting carries no grudge — it is just "hello, let's not fight." You'll get a notice telling you contact has been made, for example *"You have made contact with the French. You are at peace."*

!!! tip
    The empire-reports screen has a **Foreign** tab — your Foreign Affairs report. For every rival it shows your current stance (peace, war, or uncontacted), how many colonies and units they hold, their gold, and their military and naval strength. Their *internal* affairs — tax rate, Sons of Liberty, Founding Fathers — stay hidden. Use it to spot who is strong enough to be a threat.

## The Diplomacy screen

You have a full seat at the negotiating table. There are two ways in:

- The **Diplomacy** button on the HUD opens the negotiation screen at any time.
- A **scout that walks up to a rival's colony** is offered a short mission menu — spy on the colony for a one-off look inside, or open negotiations with its owner. (A plain soldier that walks up to the same colony just attacks it.)

### Answering a rival's offer

The computer powers make offers off their own back. A power that is losing a war with you may **sue for peace**; a strong, friendly power that shares an enemy with you may offer an **alliance**; a war grinding to a stalemate may bring a **cease-fire** offer. When a rival has proposed something this turn, the Diplomacy screen even pops up on its own at the start of your turn. You **Accept** (it takes effect at once) or **Decline** (it is simply dropped).

### Building a treaty of your own

Pick a rival you have met and assemble a treaty from any combination of clauses. When you're happy, **Submit** it and the rival weighs the whole package. If it's a fair deal for them they accept and everything settles at once. If not, they **counter** — throwing out the parts they'd never agree to and, where the only sticking point is gold they'd pay, offering to pay about half as much — and hand the trimmed deal back for you to accept or reject.

| Clause | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Pay / demand gold** | Move gold between treasuries (adjust in 100-gold steps) |
| **Give goods** | Send goods from one of your colonies' warehouses |
| **Cede / demand a colony** | Hand over one of your colonies, or take one of theirs, intact |
| **Hand over / demand a unit** | Transfer a single unit to or from the rival |
| **Incite** | Set the rival at war with a third power you have met |
| **Change of relations** | Move up or down the ladder: war → peace or cease-fire; peace → alliance; cease-fire → peace or alliance |

!!! warning
    A treaty answers you **once** — accept, a single counter, or reject — rather than haggling over many rounds the way two computer powers do between themselves. Plan your offer accordingly, and don't over-ask on gold: a demand a rival genuinely can't afford will simply be refused or halved.

## Three direct actions against a rival

Beyond treaties, the Diplomacy screen lets you act unilaterally:

- **Declare war.** When you are at peace (or under a cease-fire, or even allied) with a rival you've met, a **Declare war** button appears. There is nothing to accept — you go to war both ways at once, and the grudge spikes (declaring from a calm peace is the biggest jump). You can't "declare" on someone you've never met; attacking such a stranger on sight is what starts *that* war.
- **Trade at a foreign colony.** Once **Jan de Witt** sits in your Continental Congress (see [Founding Fathers & the Continental Congress](14-founding-fathers.md)), a ship sitting at a rival's colony can **buy and sell goods there directly**, for gold, with no involvement from Europe and no sales tax. You sell at what their market is bidding and buy at what it's asking; the trade uses up the ship's turn. You can't trade this way while at **war** with that power — make peace first. Without de Witt, the option isn't offered.
- **Demand tribute.** An armed unit standing beside a rival's colony can demand gold under threat of force. If you badly outmatch that power militarily, they pay (the more dominant you are, the more); if you're an even match or weaker, they refuse. Either way it's an act of aggression — their grudge climbs, and repeated demands can tip a fragile peace into war.

## Peace, war and the drift between them

Once you have *met* a rival and are at peace, you **cannot attack them** and they cannot attack you — the order is simply refused — until war is formally declared. A cease-fire or alliance protects you the same way.

Each turn, tension between two powers **cools** a little. A relationship follows that grudge meter: a war that has cooled enough softens to a **cease-fire**, and a cease-fire that keeps cooling drifts back to **peace**. You'll be told when this happens on its own, for example *"Your war with the Spanish has cooled to a cease-fire."*

A rival can also **turn on you by itself**. If you keep founding colonies right on a power's doorstep, its resentment builds turn after turn until it boils over into war — *"The Dutch have broken the peace — you are now at war!"* Settle a respectful distance away and a power left in peace stays peaceful; crowd it and you provoke it. This is the heart of **border friction**: land, coastline, and access to native nations are all things you and the rivals compete over.

Once at war, a rival's soldiers come after your units, march on your colonies, and will **capture an undefended colony** outright. Keep a soldier or gun garrisoned in every colony near a rival's territory.

!!! warning
    **Privateers are the exception to every rule above.** A privateer flies no flag: it can raid a rival's shipping — or be raided — **without** declaring war and without any peace treaty protecting the target. If your merchant ships start vanishing while you're nominally at peace, suspect a rival's privateer. See [Combat: Land & Naval](17-combat.md).

## Rivals, independence and score

The rivals are woven into the endgame in three ways:

1. **The independence race.** Every power is quietly racing you toward its own revolution. A rival that grows fat and strong is a rival you may have to fight — or ally with. When you finally declare independence, a sympathetic **foreign power eventually lands an allied force** to fight the King's army alongside you, and the longer the war drags on, the larger that help grows. See [The Road to Independence](18-road-to-independence.md) and [The War of Independence](19-war-of-independence.md).
2. **The last-power-standing victory.** Depending on the options chosen at setup, you can win by wiping out every rival's colonies and armies — becoming the **last European power standing**. This is an optional condition; the headline victory is still winning your War of Independence.
3. **Score.** Regions you were first to explore, the strength of your empire relative to your neighbours, and simply surviving their aggression all feed your running score. See [Winning the Game & Scoring](20-winning-scoring.md).

!!! tip
    The King can drag you *into* a war with a rival — or make peace on your behalf — whether you like it or not. **Benjamin Franklin** in your Congress keeps the King from declaring war on you and smooths your own diplomacy, making peace deals hold longer. If diplomacy matters to your strategy, he is worth pursuing.
